Learn coffee

From the plant to the cup, in order: short notes here, the app page that helps with each step, and the full KoffyKraft courses for going deeper. Figures are common ranges; your beans, roaster and water come first.
How to use the app
1 Grow
  • Arabica and Robusta want different heights, shade and care; the variety you plant sets much of the cup.
  • Living soil, shade trees and timely rain (blossom and backing showers) decide the crop more than any single input.
In the app: Crop care · Origin › varieties and words · Field log · Weather. Course: Living Root Network natural farming handbook.
2 Pick and process
  • Pick ripe cherry only; under-ripe and over-ripe fruit show up in the cup as harsh or fermented notes.
  • Washed, natural and honey are three ways of removing the fruit; each changes sweetness, body and acidity.
  • Dry slowly and evenly to a stable moisture, then rest the parchment or cherry before hulling.
In the app: Journey of the bean · Processing runs · Harvests and green lots.
3 Roast
  • Drying: the green bean loses water and turns from green to yellow. Even heat here sets up an even roast.
  • Browning: sugars and amino acids react (the Maillard reactions) and the bean turns tan to brown; most aroma starts here.
  • First crack: steam and gas split the bean with an audible crack. Ground colour at first crack sits around L* 30 on a colour meter, much the same for every arabica studied.
  • Development: the time from first crack to the end of the roast. Longer brings more sweetness and body and less acidity; too long and the coffee turns flat or bitter.
  • Second crack (around L* 20) marks dark roasts: oil comes to the surface and roast flavour takes over from origin flavour.
  • Cool fast to stop the roast, then judge the colour on ground coffee.
In the app: Timer · Graph · Roastbook · Colour check.
Course: What changes during roasting · First crack and development · Roast profiles · Roast defects · Light, medium and dark · all 25 lessons.
Colour points: UC Davis, A universal color curve for roasted arabica coffee (Scientific Reports, 2025).
4 Rest and store
  • Fresh roast gives off gas for days; most filter coffee tastes best after a few days of rest, espresso often a little longer.
  • Keep beans away from air, light, heat and damp: a valve bag or airtight jar, whole bean, ground just before brewing.
Course: Post-roast rest · Storage and packaging.
5 Cup and taste
  • Cupping is the same recipe for every coffee, so differences come from the coffee, not the brew.
  • Smell the dry grounds, break the crust, then taste as it cools: sweetness, acidity, body, aftertaste and any defect.
  • Cup each roast of the same lot side by side to see what a change in the roast did.

6 Learning to smell

Most of what we call the taste of coffee is smell. The tongue gives sweet, sour, bitter and salty; the nose gives everything else. Smell is tied to memory, so two people can smell the same thing and name it from different lives. That is not a fault. Smelling is a skill, learned like a vocabulary, and anyone can practise it.

Try this first

  • Pinch your nose and sip coffee: it turns flat, mostly bitter. Let go mid-sip and the flavour rushes back. That rush is smell, reaching the nose from the back of the mouth.
  • Brewed coffee holds too little sugar to taste, yet good coffee seems sweet. The sweetness comes largely through aroma: caramel, jaggery and fruit smells that the brain reads as sweet.

One smell, many names

Coconut oil smells of a hot kadai and home cooking to one person and of hair oil to another. Same oil, two memories. In the cup it works the same way: tulsi and clove both carry eugenol, so one taster writes "tulsi" and the next "clove". Neither is wrong.

When the table disagrees, move up a level until everyone can say yes: mulla › flower › floral; narthanga › citrus › fruity. The broader word is the one you share. Common families:

  • Floral: jasmine (mulla), coffee blossom, tulsi.
  • Fruity: citrus, ripe banana, jackfruit, raisin, tamarind.
  • Sweet: jaggery, caramel, honey, vanilla.
  • Nutty and cocoa: roasted groundnut, cashew, cocoa, dark chocolate.
  • Spice: clove, cinnamon, cardamom, black pepper, dry ginger.
  • Roasty and smoky: toast, roasted coconut, wood fire.
  • Earthy and woody: first rain on dry soil, damp wood, jute sack.
  • Fermented and sour: toddy, dosa batter, over-ripe fruit.
  • Off-notes to catch: musty, mouldy, rubbery, medicinal, burnt, onion-like or vinegary.

Indian coffee, by the nose

  • Coffee blossom after the blossom showers, in Wayanad, Coorg or Chikmagalur, smells close to jasmine. Smell it and you know the floral note in the cup.
  • Monsooned Malabar, the GI coffee from the Malabar coast, is exposed to monsoon winds until it swells and pales: low acidity, heavy body, earthy, woody and spicy notes.
  • Filter coffee with chicory: roasted chicory root adds caramel and bitterness. Smell chicory alone once, and you will find it in every blend that has it.
  • Robusta, much of India's crop, leans to cocoa, roasted grain, woody and earthy notes; a clean robusta smells of dark chocolate and nuts, a careless one of jute and earth.
  • Pepper and cardamom often grow beside coffee here. Do not expect the cup to taste of them: flavour comes from the bean, its processing and the roast. If you smell spice, the bean and roast made it.
  • On the estate: the fermentation tank, the drying yard and the store each have a smell. Learn the healthy one; a sour, mouldy or musty change is the first warning of trouble, long before the cup.

Practise: five minutes a day

  • Fill 10 to 12 small steel containers or empty spice jars from the list above. Number them; let someone else write down which is which.
  • Each day, smell a few with your eyes closed. Say the first memory that comes, then name it. Check the key. Two weeks of this changes how you cup.
  • Smell the same thing in different places: clove in the kitchen, at the spice shop, in a cup of masala chai. The more settings, the faster you know it anywhere.
  • Rest the nose: three or four samples, then a break. Smelling your own sleeve or wrist between samples resets it.
  • A cold, strong perfume, smoking or a heavy meal dulls smell. Cup in the morning, before strong food. Never sniff kerosene, pesticides or cleaning liquids to "train".

At the cupping table

  • Write your note before anyone speaks. Once someone says "jackfruit" out loud, everyone starts finding jackfruit.
  • Then share, and look for what you agree on. If one says jackfruit and another pineapple, you agree it is "tropical fruit".
  • A simple true word beats a fancy guess. "Sweet, nutty, a little citrus" is a good note.
  • Use the same words every time in the app's Cup page, so your notes can be compared across roasts and years.

7 Brew
  • Ratio, grind and water temperature are the three dials; change one at a time.
  • Water is most of the cup: clean, not too hard and not too soft.

Acidity: what you taste is not the pH

  • Coffee holds two kinds of acid. The fruit acids (citric, malic, lactic, acetic, tartaric) give the bright, juicy, lemon or green-apple feel. The chlorogenic acids are bitter-sour plant compounds that move the pH meter but say little about brightness.
  • Two coffees can share a pH and taste nothing alike, one lively, one flat. Trust the taster's notes alongside any meter.
  • The brewer changes which acids reach the cup, not just how strong it is. Paper holds some back: thick Chemex paper the most. Metal filters let more through, with oils and body.
  • Strength is not brightness. Moka and pressure brewers make a strong cup that is rich in caffeine and chlorogenic acids, not in fruit acids.
  • For a bright light roast, a washed Chikmagalur or Wayanad arabica say: V60, French press or a cloth or metal filter. For body with robusta or a dark roast: moka, South Indian filter, or village-style boiled coffee.
  • Strength and extraction move sourness most. A strong, under-extracted brew tastes sourest; a weaker, well-extracted one the least. Too sour: use a little less coffee for the water, or grind a little finer.
  • Water temperature from 87 to 93 °C barely moved the pH. The amount of acid (titratable acidity) followed brew strength and matched what tasters called sour much better than pH did.
  • Taste differs: in the same study about half of black-coffee drinkers wanted more acidity and half less. Brew for the person drinking it.
  • The bean and the roast set the limits. Brewing moves acidity within them; it cannot add fruit that the green coffee and roast did not leave.
